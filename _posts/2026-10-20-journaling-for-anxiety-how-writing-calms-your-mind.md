Anxiety is repetitive. The same worry circles back, each time slightly louder, and the circling itself becomes exhausting. You are not thinking the thought through — you are thinking it again.

Writing interrupts that. Not because the page has any special power, but because a worry has to become a specific sentence before it can be written down, and most anxious thoughts are not specific at all. They are a mood wearing the costume of a thought. Forcing one into words is often enough to show you how much of it was shape and how little was substance.

This is one of the oldest and best-supported reasons people keep a journal, and it does not require you to be a writer.

## Why writing helps

**It makes the vague concrete.** "Everything is going wrong" is unanswerable. Written down, it has to become something like "I am worried I handled that conversation badly and that it will affect my review." That version is still uncomfortable, but it is a specific thing you can actually think about.

**It gets the loop out of your head.** A worry held in your mind has to be actively maintained, and your mind keeps re-raising it because it cannot be sure you have dealt with it. Writing it down is a way of telling yourself the matter has been recorded. The loop can stop.

**It separates you from the thought.** Reading a worry in your own handwriting or on a screen puts a small distance between you and it. You move from being inside the anxiety to looking at it. That shift is most of the relief.

**It shows you your patterns.** This is the part that only works over time. A month of entries will show you that your anxiety spikes on Sunday evenings, or before a particular kind of meeting, or when you have not slept. Patterns are manageable in a way that free-floating dread is not.

**It gives you evidence.** Anxiety is a bad forecaster, but it is also confident, so you rarely check its record. A journal is a record. Reading back what you were certain would go wrong three months ago, and what actually happened, is a genuinely useful corrective.

## Four ways to write when you are anxious

You do not need a method, but these help when you do not know where to start.

**Write it out without stopping.** Set a timer for ten minutes and write continuously about whatever is bothering you. Do not organise it, do not punctuate it properly, do not try to reach a conclusion. The aim is to empty, not to compose. This is the most reliable way to take the pressure down quickly.

**Name the worry, then question it.** Write the fear as one plain sentence. Underneath, answer three things: what is the actual evidence for this, what would I tell a friend who said it, and what is the realistic worst case and could I handle it? This is the written version of something therapists do out loud, and it works for the same reasons.

**Write the worst case all the way through.** Counter-intuitively, following a fear to its end tends to defuse it. Most catastrophes shrink when you describe them in detail, because the description reveals the parts you would survive.

**Write three good things.** On days when examining the anxiety would only feed it, do not. Write down three things that went well instead, however small. Gratitude journaling is a shift of attention rather than an analysis, and sometimes that is exactly what is needed.

## Keeping it going

Two things determine whether this becomes a habit.

**Write when the anxiety is high, not on a schedule.** Journaling for anxiety is a tool, not a discipline. The right time is when you need it. If that is three times in one bad week and not at all the next, that is correct use.

**Make sure it is genuinely private.** This matters more here than in any other kind of journaling. If some part of you is aware that the entry might be read, you will write a tidied-up version, and the tidied-up version does not help. You need to be able to put down the thought you would not say aloud.

This is where an online journal has a real advantage over a notebook. There is nothing to find in a drawer, nothing to leave on a train, and it is with you at the moment you need it — at 2am, or on the bus before the thing you are dreading. Being private by default means you can write the unflattering, frightened, unreasonable version, which is the one that actually needs to come out.

There is also something to be said for not being alone with it. Writing a difficult entry and finding that other people have written almost the same thing is a particular kind of relief that a private notebook cannot offer. Being able to keep everything private but occasionally share one entry gives you both — the honesty of a locked journal, and the option of being heard.

## One important caveat

Journaling helps a great many people manage anxiety. It is not a treatment for an anxiety disorder, and it is not a substitute for talking to a doctor or a therapist. If your anxiety is persistent, getting worse, or interfering with your work, sleep or relationships, please speak to a professional. A journal is a genuinely useful thing to bring to that conversation — it is a record of what has been happening and when — but it works best alongside real support, not instead of it.

## Start with one page

Tonight, write down the thing that is bothering you. One paragraph, no structure, nobody reading it. You will probably find that it was smaller on the page than it was in your head.
