title: What to write in a journal when you don't know what to write
---

<p class="disclosure"><strong>Disclosure:</strong> we run Goodnight Journal, which is mentioned near the end. The techniques here work in any journal, including a paper one.</p>

<p>You opened the journal. The page is blank. You cannot think of anything, and the longer you sit there the more it feels like you have nothing in your life worth recording.</p>

<p>This is the single most common reason people stop journaling, and it is almost always caused by the same mistake: <b>you are trying to write something worth reading.</b></p>

<p>Nobody is going to read it. That is the whole point of the format. A journal is not a piece of writing, it is thinking with your hands, and the quality bar is zero. Once you actually believe that, the blank page stops being intimidating — but believing it takes a few sessions of writing badly on purpose.</p>

<p>Here is what to do in the meantime.</p>

<h2>Start boringly, on purpose</h2>

<p>Write what you did today, in order, in flat detail. Got up late. Burnt the toast. The meeting was fine. Walked home the long way because it was still light.</p>

<p>It feels pointless while you are doing it, and then somewhere around the fourth sentence you will write something you did not expect — <i>walked home the long way because I did not want to be in the flat yet</i> — and that is the entry. The boring account is scaffolding. You are not writing it for itself, you are writing it to find the thing underneath.</p>

<p>This works more reliably than any prompt, and it costs nothing to try.</p>

<h2>Write the thing you are avoiding</h2>

<p>There is usually something. The conversation you need to have, the decision you keep deferring, the worry you have not looked at directly.</p>

<p>If you know what it is and you are writing around it, write it instead. One sentence is enough to start: <i>I think I need to leave this job.</i> Then keep going, because having written it down, you will find you have more to say than you thought.</p>

<p>If you do not know what you are avoiding, write "I don't know why I feel off today" and keep going for ten minutes. You will often arrive at it by accident.</p>

<h2>Answer a question instead of facing a page</h2>

<p>A question is much easier to respond to than a blank page, because it gives you somewhere to push against. Keep three or four you can fall back on:</p>

<ul>
  <li>What was the best ten minutes of today?</li>
  <li>What am I putting off, and what is the actual reason?</li>
  <li>What would I tell a friend in my situation right now?</li>
  <li>What do I want to remember about this week in five years?</li>
</ul>

<p>If you want a longer supply, we have <a href="/20-journaling-prompts-to-boost-your-success/">20 journaling prompts</a> and <a href="/10-journal-writing-prompts-to-make-you-happy/">10 prompts to make you happy</a>. Use them as a crutch for as long as you need one; plenty of people journal from prompts for years and get everything out of it they wanted.</p>

<h2>Write a list instead of prose</h2>

<p>Nobody said entries have to be paragraphs. Five things that annoyed me. Three things I am looking forward to. Everything currently on my mind, in no order.</p>

<p>Lists are much easier to start than sentences, and they count. Some of the most useful entries you will ever write are lists.</p>

<h2>Describe one thing in front of you</h2>

<p>When nothing internal is available, go external. Describe the room, the view, the person across from you on the train, the way the light is at this time of year.</p>

<p>This sounds like a writing exercise rather than journaling, and it is — but it does two things. It gets words moving, and in ten years those details will bring a period of your life back far more vividly than any summary of your feelings about it.</p>

<h2>Write about why you do not want to write</h2>

<p>Genuinely. "I don't want to do this. It feels self-indulgent and I'm tired and I'd rather watch something." That is a real entry about a real state of mind, and it very often turns into a better one by the third sentence.</p>

<h2>Three rules that make all of the above easier</h2>

<p><b>Set a timer for ten minutes and do not stop.</b> Not to write for ten minutes, but to remove the decision about when you are finished. Most blank-page paralysis is really decision paralysis.</p>

<p><b>Do not reread while you are writing.</b> Editing and generating cannot happen at once. Rereading is how a half-finished entry becomes an abandoned one.</p>

<p><b>Two sentences counts as a complete entry.</b> The habit matters more than the length, and a journal with a hundred two-sentence entries is worth more than one with four beautiful essays and an eight-month gap.</p>

<h2>Let the app do some of the work</h2>

<p>If the blank page is a recurring problem rather than an occasional one, lean on tooling.</p>

<p>Some journals offer prompts on demand, which removes the decision entirely. Apple's built-in Journal app on iPhone goes further and suggests entries from your day — the photos you took, where you went, what you did — so you start from something that actually happened instead of from nothing.</p>

<p>In <a href="https://www.goodnightjournal.com/">Goodnight Journal</a> there is a prompt feature for the same reason, and one thing the others cannot offer: you can read entries other journal writers have chosen to publish. Reading someone else write plainly about an ordinary bad week is often the thing that unlocks your own entry, because it resets what you think a journal is supposed to sound like.</p>

<h2>The short version</h2>

<p>Write what you did today, flatly, until something surprises you. Set a timer. Do not reread. Two sentences is enough.</p>

<p>And if tonight all you manage is "nothing to say, tired, going to bed" — that is a journal entry, and tomorrow is easier for having written it.</p>
