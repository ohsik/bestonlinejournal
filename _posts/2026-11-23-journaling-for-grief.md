<p>Most writing about grief journaling is unbearable to read when you are actually grieving. It talks about journeys and healing and closure, and none of those words describe anything you currently recognise.</p>

<p>So here is the plain version: writing things down helps some people after a death, in a few specific and fairly limited ways. It will not make this smaller. What it can do is keep hold of things you are frightened of losing, and give you somewhere to put the parts you cannot say out loud.</p>

<h2>There is no right way, and no schedule</h2>

<p>Worth clearing this out of the way first, because it causes real harm.</p>

<p>You have probably heard about the five stages of grief. Those came from work with people who were themselves dying, not with the bereaved, and they were never meant as a sequence mourners pass through on a timetable. Grief does not go in order, it does not finish, and it comes back on ordinary Tuesdays years later for no reason at all.</p>

<p>This matters for journaling specifically, because if you believe there is a correct trajectory you will start reading your own entries as a progress report and finding yourself behind. There is nothing to be behind on.</p>

<h2>Write the details down now — this one is urgent</h2>

<p>If you only do one thing from this page, do this one, and do it soon.</p>

<p>The large facts stay. What goes, and faster than anyone expects, are the small specific ones: the exact way they said your name, how they held a mug, the phrase they used instead of goodbye, what their handwriting looked like, how they laughed when something was genuinely funny versus politely.</p>

<p>Those are the things people say they would give anything to have written down, and they fade within months without you noticing it happening. You do not need to write well. A list of fragments is enough:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Things they always said</li>
  <li>What their hands looked like</li>
  <li>Food they made, and how</li>
  <li>Opinions they held that nobody else held</li>
  <li>How they were on an ordinary morning, not at their best or worst</li>
</ul>

<p>Add to it whenever something surfaces. It will surface at inconvenient times — write it down then, badly, on your phone.</p>

<h2>Write to them, not about them</h2>

<p>This sounds strange until you try it, and then it is often the thing that works.</p>

<p>Rather than writing an account of your feelings, write to the person. Tell them what happened this week. Tell them the thing you would have phoned them about. Argue with them. Tell them about the funeral, or that their grandchild did something they would have found funny.</p>

<p>This is not pretending they are alive. It is continuing a relationship that has changed form rather than ended, which is what most people do anyway, internally, for the rest of their lives. Putting it on a page makes it less lonely, and it sidesteps the impossible task of summarising how you feel.</p>

<h2>Write the parts you cannot say out loud</h2>

<p>Grief is not only sadness, and the other feelings are the ones that get no airtime because they sound shameful said aloud.</p>

<p>Relief, if the end was long or the relationship was hard. Anger at the person who died. Anger at people who are being kind badly. Boredom with your own grief. Guilt about the fortnight you did not think about them much. Resentment at everyone whose life carried on.</p>

<p>All of that is ordinary and almost nobody says it, which leaves people convinced they are grieving wrongly. A private journal is the one place it can go without managing anyone else's reaction. This is the strongest argument for writing it down rather than only talking about it.</p>

<h2>Mark the dates</h2>

<p>Anniversaries, birthdays, the first Christmas, the day it happened. These land harder than the ordinary days and they take people by surprise, sometimes as a bad week with no apparent cause.</p>

<p>Note what you did and how it went. Partly so you can see the pattern — the dread before the date is often worse than the date — and partly because in five years you will want to know how you got through the first one.</p>

<h2>Two things not to do</h2>

<p><b>Do not use it to measure progress.</b> Rereading January from June to check whether you are improving is a reliable way to feel worse. Grief is not linear and the comparison is meaningless.</p>

<p><b>Do not make yourself conclude anything.</b> Entries can stop mid-thought. There is no requirement to arrive at acceptance, a lesson, or a tidy ending, and forcing one produces writing that is false and does not help.</p>

<h2>When to get help</h2>

<p>Grief is not an illness and it does not need treating. Most people, with time and people around them, do not need professional help to mourn.</p>

<p>But some do, and it is worth knowing the difference. If after a long time nothing has shifted at all — you cannot function, cannot accept what happened, or life has stayed entirely stopped — that is recognised and treatable, and a doctor is the right call. Bereavement counselling helps many people long before it gets to that point, and wanting it is not a sign you are grieving badly.</p>

<p><b>And if you are having thoughts of harming yourself, please contact emergency services or a crisis line where you live, today.</b> That is not something to write your way through alone.</p>

<h2>Where to keep it</h2>

<p>A notebook is completely fine and many people prefer one here.</p>

<p>The case for something digital is narrow: the details arrive at odd moments and a phone is already in your hand, and it is searchable later when you want to find the one entry about a particular afternoon. If you want that, <a href="https://www.goodnightjournal.com/">Goodnight Journal</a> keeps entries private by default, which matters more than usual with this material.</p>

<p>It also lets you publish a single entry if you ever want to, and some people find that reading other people writing plainly about their own loss is the first thing that makes them feel less strange. Others would hate that. Both are reasonable.</p>

<h2>If you take one thing</h2>

<p>Write down the small details while you still have them. Everything else on this page can wait, or not happen at all. That part cannot.</p>
