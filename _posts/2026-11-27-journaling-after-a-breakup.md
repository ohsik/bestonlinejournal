<p>The thing that makes a breakup different from other kinds of loss is that the person is still here. They are alive, they are reachable, they are probably visible on a screen, and some part of your brain knows the whole thing could be undone with one message.</p>

<p>That is the specific problem a journal is good for. Not processing, not closure — somewhere to put the things you would otherwise do.</p>

<h2>Write the message you are not going to send</h2>

<p>This is the single most useful thing on this page.</p>

<p>Write it properly. All of it — the furious version, the pleading version, the devastatingly reasonable one where you explain exactly what they did and they finally understand. Put it in the journal with the date on it, and do not send it.</p>

<p>Two things happen. The urge drops considerably, because most of what you wanted was to say it rather than for them to receive it. And in three days you can read it back, which is the most reliable cure there is for believing it was a good idea.</p>

<p>Do this as often as you need to. Some people write a dozen of these in the first month. That is a dozen messages not sent, which is a real result.</p>

<h2>Write down what was actually wrong — now, while you still remember</h2>

<p>This one is time-sensitive and almost nobody does it.</p>

<p>Right now you can probably recall precisely what was difficult: the specific argument that kept happening, the way certain evenings went, the thing you stopped mentioning to friends. In two months, if it is going badly, your memory will quietly edit all of that out and leave only the good parts. Nostalgia is an unreliable narrator and it works fast.</p>

<p>So write the plain list while it is accurate. Not as an exercise in bitterness — as a record. If there is ever a conversation about trying again, your six-weeks-ago self is a far more credible witness than your lonely Tuesday-night self, and you will be glad to have the testimony on paper.</p>

<p>The same applies in reverse, for the same reason. If you catch yourself writing only that they were terrible, write down what was genuinely good too. Both the demonising and the idealising are distortions, and the usefulness of a dated journal is that it catches you doing each in turn.</p>

<h2>Somewhere for the checking</h2>

<p>You know the urge. Looking at their profile, working out who that is in the photo, drafting a reply to something they did not address to you.</p>

<p>When it arrives, write the entry instead: what you wanted to look for, and what you were hoping or dreading to find. It takes about ninety seconds and it interrupts the loop often enough to be worth the habit. It also leaves you a record of how often it is happening, which in a month's time will be the clearest evidence that it is happening less.</p>

<h2>It gets boring, and that is the sign</h2>

<p>At some point you will sit down to write and find you have nothing much to say about it. The entry will be two flat lines, and you will feel almost cheated.</p>

<p>That is what recovery looks like from the inside. Not a breakthrough — just the subject gradually becoming less interesting than everything else. A journal is the only thing that will show you it happened, because you will not notice at the time, and in a year you will not be able to remember when it stopped hurting.</p>

<h2>Two traps</h2>

<p><b>Rehearsing instead of writing.</b> There is a difference between putting something down and going round it again. If entries are getting longer, saying the same thing, and leaving you more wound up than when you started, the journal has joined the problem. Keep writing but make entries short and factual for a few weeks — what happened, what you did, nothing analysed.</p>

<p><b>Writing for them.</b> If some part of you is composing in case they ever read it, you are not journaling, you are drafting. The test is whether you would write the same sentence if you knew with certainty that nobody ever would.</p>

<h2>If the relationship was abusive</h2>

<p>Then two things change.</p>

<p>Privacy stops being a preference and becomes a safety matter. A notebook someone can find, or an account they know the password to, is a risk — use something they have no access to, and consider whether anything on a shared device or plan is really private.</p>

<p>And a dated record of what happened can matter later in ways that are hard to predict. Keep it somewhere safe, and please talk to a domestic abuse support service in your country rather than working it out alone. That is outside what any article, or any journal, can help with.</p>

<h2>When it is more than a breakup</h2>

<p>Breakups are supposed to hurt, for longer than people admit, and needing months is ordinary rather than a sign of weakness.</p>

<p>But if you are not sleeping or eating, cannot work, or it is not shifting at all after a long stretch, that can tip into depression, which is treatable and worth seeing a doctor about. Our <a href="/journaling-for-depression/">journaling for depression</a> page covers what writing can and cannot do there. <b>And if you are having thoughts of harming yourself, please contact emergency services or a crisis line where you live, today.</b></p>

<h2>Where to keep it</h2>

<p>Paper is fine. The argument for digital here is mostly about the two time-sensitive uses above: the unsent message and the checking urge both arrive at 11pm with your phone in your hand, not at a desk.</p>

<p>Privacy matters more than usual with this material, for obvious reasons. If you want something private by default with a lock on the app, <a href="https://www.goodnightjournal.com/">Goodnight Journal</a> does that. Some people also find that reading other people writing plainly about the same thing helps more than any advice does; others would rather not. Both are fine.</p>

<h2>Tonight</h2>

<p>Write the message you are not going to send. Date it. Leave it there.</p>
